AI Scam Awareness

AI has made scams faster to run and far more convincing — cloned voices, fake videos, and near-perfect phishing messages. This page is free for anyone to read and share.

Jump to the Checklist

Why AI has changed the game

Scams used to be easier to spot: bad grammar, an obviously fake voice, a stranger's photo. AI tools now let scammers write flawless messages in seconds, clone a real person's voice from a few seconds of audio, and generate fake video calls. The old warning signs still matter, but they're no longer enough on their own.

Common AI-enabled scams

Voice cloning ("family emergency")

A cloned voice of a relative or boss calls in distress, urgently asking for money or gift cards. The voice can sound completely convincing.

Deepfake video calls

A fabricated video of an executive or official asks an employee to urgently transfer funds or share credentials.

AI-written phishing

Emails and texts with no typos or awkward phrasing, closely mimicking a real bank, vendor, or coworker.

Fake AI investment bots

"Guaranteed returns" trading bots or crypto schemes that claim to use AI to beat the market.

Fake job postings

AI-generated job ads and recruiter messages that lead to upfront "fees" or requests for personal/banking details.

Romance & chatbot scams

AI chatbots sustain long, personalized conversations to build trust before asking for money.

Fake customer support

Convincing fake "AI support agents" on lookalike websites or phone lines that harvest logins or payment details.

Fake reviews & news

AI-generated reviews, testimonials, and fake news articles used to make a scam look legitimate.

Red flags — regardless of how convincing it looks or sounds

  • Urgency or pressure to act right now, before you can think or verify.
  • Requests to pay by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency.
  • Asks you to keep the request secret or bypass normal approval steps.
  • Contact comes through an unexpected channel (a call, text, or DM out of nowhere).
  • Any request to share a one-time passcode, password, or MFA code.
  • Returns or income that sound too good to be true.
  • You can't verify the person's identity through a separate, known channel.

How to protect yourself and your business

Verify on a second channel

If a call, email, or video seems urgent or unusual, hang up and contact the person directly using a number or address you already know — not one they gave you.

Set a family/team code word

Agree on a word only real family members or staff would know, to use if someone claims to be them in an emergency.

Never bypass MFA

No legitimate person or company will ever ask you to read them a one-time code.

Slow down on urgency

Real emergencies rarely require an instant, unverifiable payment. Pressure is itself a warning sign.

Verify companies independently

Look up a business or recruiter's official site and contact them directly, rather than trusting links or numbers in the message.

Train your team

Make sure staff know these tactics exist and have a clear process for verifying unusual requests before acting.

Printable scam-check checklist

Use this before acting on any unexpected request for money, credentials, or urgent action. Your browser's print/save-as-PDF option works well here.

If you think you've been targeted or scammed

Stop contact with the scammer, do not send further payment, and report it. In the United States: reportfraud.ftc.gov (Federal Trade Commission) and ic3.gov (FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center). Outside the US, report to your local consumer protection agency, police, or national cybercrime reporting center. If money was sent by bank transfer or card, also contact your bank or card issuer immediately.
